Dr. Jack Kevorkian, aka "Dr. Death", was most well known for his strong advocacy for [[assisted suicide]] for the terminally ill and his practice of facilitating them. By his own account, he helped over 130 people die between 1990 and 1998.


<details>
  <summary>Criminal Trials (1994–1999)</summary>
  
  Dr. Jack Kevorkian faced five criminal trials between 1994 and 1999, with three acquittals, one mistrial, and one conviction.

  <details>
    <summary>1994: People v. Kevorkian (Thomas Hyde)</summary>
    <ul>
      <li>Charged in the 1993 death of 30-year-old Thomas Hyde.</li>
      <li>Hyde was expected to die naturally of [[ALS (Lou Gerhig's disease)]] within weeks of his suicide.</li>
    </ul>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>March 1996: People v. Kevorkian (Frederick & Khalili)</summary>
    <ul>
      <li>Charged in the 1993 deaths of 72-year-old Merian Ruth Frederick and 61-year-old Dr. Ali Khalili.</li>
      <li>Frederick was a terminal ALS patient; Khalili was dying of bone cancer.</li>
    </ul>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>May 1996: People v. Kevorkian (Wantz & Miller)</summary>
    <ul>
      <li>Charged in the deaths of Marjoriee Wantz and Sherri Miller.</li>
    </ul>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>1997: People v. Kevorkian (Loretta Peabody)</summary>
    <ul>
      <li>Charged in the 1993 death of Loretta Peabody.</li>
    </ul>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>1999: People v. Kevorkian (Thomas Youk)</summary>
    <ul>
      <li>Charged in the 1998 death of Thomas Youk.</li>
      <li><strong>Result:</strong> This trial led to Kevorkian's second-degree murder conviction.</li>
    </ul>
  </details>

</details>


Dr. Kevorkian had noble intentions – to provide a decent and dignified exit from suffering for the terminally ill. His unfortunate decision to allow his ego to guide him made his antics, rather than his philosophy, the star of the show.

In November 1998, Dr. Kevorkian made an extraordinarily bold and public challenge to his opposers when he broadcast the videotaped death of Thomas Youk on *60 Minutes*.  The video shows Kevorkian administering the lethal injection to Youk. 

This was  key difference between the first four trials and the last. His involvement in the patient's suicide was direct, no assistive, and the resulting criminal charge was murder amongst others. 


Without dismissing the fact that he did commit a crime in this instance, his public taunt of the District Attorney and his subsequent decision to represent himself in court was what convicted him. The result was his inability to do the very thing he was fighting for -- he stifled his own ability to aid the suffering.

The discourse involving Dr. Kevorkian was assisted suicide, but the more salient matter is the right of an individual to choose for him or herself. [[Personal  Autonomy in Modern Society]]
